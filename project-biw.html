Overview

As a Manufacturing Equipment Engineering Intern supporting Model 3 Body-in-White, I worked on production-adjacent mechanical tooling and fixture problems where the design has to survive real shop-floor constraints: repeatability, serviceability, fabrication path, dimensional verification, and handoff quality.

The strongest theme across my BIW work is moving from CAD output to production readiness: understanding the problem, translating engineer feedback into design changes, documenting decisions, and building a path from concept to fabricated and validated tooling.

Stud-Gun TCP Certification Fixture

I am designing a checking/certification fixture for stud-gun TCP alignment. The problem is not simply creating a part; the fixture has to help certify repeatable tool positioning, support dimensional verification, and be usable by the team after I leave.

  • Created a multi-part fixture concept in CATIA V5 for one-time certification of a reference gun and comparison of other guns against that certified condition.
  • Used standard locating-block logic, shim adjustability, and dowel locking so the final certified position can be adjusted, verified, and preserved.
  • Working with dimensional engineering to challenge assumptions around datum strategy, repeatability, access, and what must be measured before the design is frozen.
  • Building the project toward a physical deliverable: requirements, CAD, drawings, review feedback, fabrication path, fit check, validation plan, and final handoff package.

Reverse-Engineered Production Hardware

A legacy clamping fixture system had no usable supplier drawing package available, creating a long-lead-time and high-cost replacement risk. I reverse-engineered the system in CATIA V5 and produced internal-machining drawings so the team would not be locked into the original vendor path.

  • Captured geometry from physical hardware and rebuilt the fixture system as a usable CATIA model and drawing package.
  • Created a lasting internal asset for future machining instead of relying on supplier availability.
  • For the immediate need, supported the lower-cost repair path rather than over-building new hardware when replacement was not necessary.
  • Reduced long-lead vendor dependency and avoided a five-figure external replacement cost.
Manufacturing Equipment Learning System

Because my time is split across BIW and Optimus work, I treat passdowns, line walks, and engineer conversations as an intentional learning system. Instead of trying to absorb everything, I track patterns and ask targeted questions around real equipment issues.

What I track
Recurring issue themes, likely failure modes, mechanical vs. controls boundaries, temporary vs. permanent countermeasures, and open questions for engineers.
What it builds
Production awareness, better design judgment, practical troubleshooting vocabulary, and stronger questions during reviews and floor conversations.
Production Readiness Package

For the stud-gun fixture, the final deliverable is being structured as a build-and-handoff package rather than only a CAD file. The package is intended to make the fixture understandable to another engineer, machinist, technician, or reviewer after my internship.

Build package
Requirements, CAD/drawings, material or fabrication notes, assembly logic, decision log, open questions, and owner list.
Validation package
Fit-check plan, repeatability/dimensional checks, user-feedback notes, pass/fail criteria, and final next steps.
What This Demonstrates
  • Ownership beyond CAD: requirements, stakeholder feedback, build path, validation, and final handoff.
  • Manufacturing judgment: choosing repair or internal machining when that is more practical than replacing hardware.
  • Dimensional awareness: designing around repeatability, adjustment, datums, and verification instead of only nominal geometry.
  • Execution discipline: documenting decisions and open issues so the project can keep moving even when people are busy.
Lessons Learned
Production readiness is the real deliverable
A fixture is not done when the CAD looks clean. It is done when the team understands how it is built, checked, adjusted, used, maintained, and handed off.
Dimensional feedback changes the design
The dimensional review forces the fixture to answer harder questions: what is the reference, how repeatable is the setup, what can move, and what proves the final condition is acceptable?
Cost savings can come from judgment, not just redesign
The best engineering move is sometimes creating the drawing package and choosing a smaller repair path for the immediate problem, while leaving the team with a stronger long-term option.
