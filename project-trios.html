The Problem
3-person setup is not scalable

Current Optimus training data collection on the Model 3 Body Line requires a 3-person team per station: one operator performs the production task while two people stand at the station holding camera frames to capture hand motion and body movement. The operator also wears a battery/sensor module with a helmet-mounted sensor. This works but cannot scale — you can’t staff two people per station across a full production line.

The operator workflow that needs to be captured includes: picking parts from racks or bins, moving with the part, placing into conveyors or fixtures, performing finger swipe or HMI confirmation actions, waiting through the station cycle, picking the completed part, and returning it to the rack. Any mounting solution needs to cover all of these views without creating a trip hazard, blocking the operator, or requiring the station to be taken offline to install.

System Architecture

The concept groups 12 cameras into three 4-camera pods. Each pod has its own local junction node for cable management, a support structure that clamps to existing fixed station features, and short rigid boom arms to position each camera. Three cable groups — one per pod — route back to a central battery/module sled, replacing the current 12 individual loose cables.

Coverage Requirements

The system needs to capture multiple synchronized angles covering the full operator workflow:

  • Close hand/fixture views — detailed finger and hand motion during part placement and confirmation swipes
  • Wide body motion views — full operator posture and movement envelope through the cycle
  • Pick/rack views — part retrieval from bins and racks at cycle start
  • Carry-path views — operator movement with part from pick to place
  • Return-to-rack views — part return or completed part removal at cycle end
  • HMI/finger-swipe views — operator interface confirmation actions
Pilot Station — FDL-005

The next pilot target is FDL-005, a guarded spot-welder robot/fixture station. This station was chosen because it introduces realistic production-floor constraints: fixed fencing and robot guarding (mounting candidates), operator mats, movable bins and racks (mounting exclusions), E-stops and gates (must not contact), overhead structures, and limited walking space.

The station layout is being modeled in CATIA V5 to support design review before physical implementation. The model includes station fencing, fixture geometry, storage bins, and proposed pod locations. Additional elements being added include camera fields of view, operator working envelope, robot/no-go zones, cable routing paths, junction node locations, and bracket concepts.

  • An early prototype was tested on an RRT conveyor using an existing 80/20 frame with temporary tripod/GoPro-style mounts — validated the concept but exposed repeatability and rigidity issues
  • Current design direction: standardized lockable mounts clamped to existing guard/fence structures — no new full-station 80/20 required
  • Key open item: cable slack management for ~20ft cables without creating trip hazards or production interference

Lessons Learned
Temporary mounts are a false start, not a proof of concept
The RRT conveyor prototype validated that station-mounted cameras can capture the workflow. But GoPro-style temporary mounts drift, can’t be locked repeatably, and aren’t scalable. The prototype answered "can this work" — the real engineering question is "how do you make it work the same way every time across every station type."
Cable management is a first-class constraint, not an afterthought
20ft of cable per camera × 12 cameras is a serious production-floor hazard if not managed. The architecture decision to group into three managed bundles was driven entirely by the cable problem, not by camera coverage. Good system design means solving the constraint that will actually kill you in production, not the one that looks cleanest on paper.
CATIA modeling before physical install saves rework
FDL-005 has enough geometric complexity — fencing, robot guarding, bins, mats, E-stops — that installing without a model would mean discovering conflicts physically. Modeling the station in CATIA first lets you identify mounting conflicts, cable routing dead-ends, and operator clearance issues in a design review before anyone touches the production floor.
