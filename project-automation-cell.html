Why I'm Building It
The gap I wanted to close

On a production floor I worked on tooling that a robot and a PLC already drove — I owned the mechanical end of the problem, not the sequencing, sensing, or fault handling around it. This project is the deliberate inverse: a cell small enough that I own every layer of it, from the locating scheme on the part to the state machine that decides when the gripper is allowed to close.

The whole cell is built around one pick object — an 8 × 22 × 7 mm 608ZZ bearing — located by an ID pin and rest at the pickup pose and a different datum scheme at the destination. Fixing the part first, then designing everything else to serve it, is the same order of operations that industrial fixturing follows, and it is what keeps the scope from drifting.

The single statement that decides whether this project worked:

Success criterion

The system shall autonomously detect, pick, transfer, and place a part for 50 consecutive cycles with at least 98% successful completion, documented fault handling, and measured positional repeatability.

Current CAD — Revision B

The prototype is a 4-DOF articulated arm: base yaw, shoulder pitch, elbow pitch, and a single-finger gripper, driven by three SG90 micro servos and one MG996R at the shoulder. It is modelled natively in SolidWorks 2026 as editable parts and a real mated assembly — not a static arrangement of meshes — so joint travel can actually be exercised in CAD.

Design Decisions
Joint Architecture
Servo horn takes torque only
608ZZ bearings take radial load
6.35 × 25.4 mm steel dowels as pivots
Printed dowel-to-8 mm-bore adapters
Screwed keepers for axial retention
Serviceability
Removable yaw servo cassette
Positive yaw crossbolt on the D-collar
Tool access checked per fastener
Screw-captured TPU jaw pads
M2.5 at the shoulder ears — larger nut corners foul the servo case
Sizing Basis
20 g payload target (provisional)
Solid-density mass bound, 1.25 load factor
Budget = ⅓ of reference stall torque
Shoulder margin 2.15× · elbow 1.44×
Reference ratings, not measured torque
  • The bearing does the work the horn shouldn't. A servo output shaft is not a structural bearing. Every pitch joint pairs the horn with a dowel running in a 608ZZ, so the horn only ever sees torque — the reason the joints grew cheeks, standoffs and keepers instead of bolting a link straight to a horn.
  • A 3:1 geared SG90 shoulder was evaluated and rejected — restricted travel and too little load margin. The MG996R was chosen on travel and margin, and adds an estimated $15–30 to the build.
  • Joint travel was verified in the native model, not eyeballed. A separate configuration suppresses the command mates and enforces angle limits: yaw ±60°, shoulder −30° to +80°, elbow −100° to −5°, grip 0–25°. Nine poses and twelve transfer samples solved with zero solid overlaps.
  • Those checks are discrete samples, not a swept proof. Continuous sweep, cable routing, and external fixtures are still excluded — which is why the first pickup pose I tried (−40°/−50°) turned out to clip the yaw platform and had to be revised to −30°/−60°.
  • Structure is PETG, jaw pads are TPU. Printed at the WashU makerspace; a dedicated fit coupon is exported alongside the parts so the dowel and bearing fits get trialled before a full set is printed.
Open Problem — Horn Interface
Fabrication hold · caught in CAD, before ordering

The shoulder is modelled against a nominal 26 mm horn plate on a 20 mm bolt circle. The metal disc horn actually sold for this servo class is 20 mm OD on a 14 mm bolt circle, 4.5 mm thick — it will not drop into the modelled pattern, and substituting it would also shift the axial stack by about 2.5 mm. The SG90 horns need the same treatment: the model assumes an 8 mm hole radius and M2 through-fasteners, the supplier drawing shows 8.3 mm and roughly 1 mm pilots.

Every horn-connected part is held from release until a real horn is selected and measured, the link pattern, washer relief and screw engagement are updated, and the affected mates are re-solved. Parts that do not touch a horn are unaffected. This is the cheapest possible place to find that mismatch — the alternative is finding it with a printed part and a bag of the wrong screws.

Plan to Validation

Four phases, each independently demonstrable. No phase starts before the previous one has measured results — the failure mode for a project like this is fragmenting it into five half-finished subsystems.

PhaseGoalDemonstrable outputStatus
1 — Mechanical proof Printed arm, one pick object, defined pickup and place poses, repeatable hard stops Manual cycle with basic repeatability data CAD in progress
2 — Motion & sensing Arduino motion control, home switch, part-present sensor, end-position confirmation Repeatable automatic pick-and-place cycle Not started
3 — PLC supervisory control Micro820 owns cycle start, permissives, state sequence, faults, reset and the handshake; the motion controller owns low-level movement Fault injected, detected, reported, recovered Not started
4 — Validation Repeatability, pick rate, cycle time, fault recovery, payload, gripper retention, sensor robustness Completed test report Not started

The PLC-to-motion-controller split is deliberate. Letting the PLC supervise a separate motion controller over a simple I/O handshake is how a real cell is partitioned, and it is the part of automation I had the least ownership of on the floor.

What I've Learned So Far
Lock the part before you design the machine
Choosing the 608ZZ first — and writing down how it gets located at both ends of the cycle — is what made every later decision decidable. Jaw geometry, payload, Z clearance over the pin, and the sensor choice all fall out of that one commitment. The scope only started converging once the part stopped being a variable.
A model that can't move hasn't been checked
Building real mates instead of a fixed arrangement is slower, and it is what caught the pickup pose colliding with the yaw platform. Driving the joints through named angle dimensions and then independently comparing all 199 component transforms against the kinematics turns "it looks fine" into a check that either passes or does not.
Separate what you assumed from what you measured
The load report labels every input: CAD solid volume, manufacturer reference mass, assumed friction coefficient. The torque margins are real conclusions from those inputs, but they are not test data, and writing them down that way keeps me from quoting a 2.15× margin as if a servo had actually been loaded. The same discipline is why the Results table above is still blank.
